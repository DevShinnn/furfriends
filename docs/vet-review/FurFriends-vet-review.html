A. Emergency first aid

Shown on the Emergency page. Owners use these steps while getting to a vet, so check that nothing could delay care or cause harm.

A1

“Go to a vet now if you see”

  • Struggling to breathe, or pale, blue or grey gums
  • Collapse, can't stand, or won't wake up properly
  • A seizure lasting over 5 minutes, or more than one in a day
  • Swollen, tight belly with retching but nothing coming up
  • Straining to pee with little or nothing coming out (especially male cats)
  • Ate or touched something poisonous, even if they seem fine
  • Bleeding that won't stop after 5 minutes of firm pressure
  • Hit by a vehicle or fell from a height
  • Heavy panting, drooling and weakness on a hot day
  • Any eye injury, or an eye bulging out of its socket

Pet first-aid kit

  • Your vet's number and the nearest 24-hour animal hospital
  • Gauze pads, rolled gauze and self-stick bandage wrap
  • Blunt-tip scissors, tweezers and a tick remover
  • Sterile saline for rinsing eyes and wounds

  • Instant cold pack
  • Needle-free syringe for rinsing or giving water

A4

Heatstroke

Go to a vet now Heat & burns

Overheating can damage organs within minutes. Flat-faced and thick-coated breeds are most at risk.

Signs to look for

  • Heavy panting, or noisy breathing (any panting in a cat is a warning sign)
  • Thick drool, bright red or very pale gums
  • Vomiting or diarrhea
  • Wobbly, weak or confused; collapse or seizures

Don't

  • Ice or ice-cold water. It narrows blood vessels and slows cooling.
  • Leaving a wet towel draped over them. It traps heat. Wet the coat and keep air moving instead.
  • Waiting to see if they recover on their own.

What to do now

  1. Move them out of the sun right away, into shade or an air-conditioned room.
  2. Wet the coat with cool tap water, especially the belly, armpits, groin and paws, then fan them so the water evaporates.
  3. Offer small sips of cool water if they're alert. Don't force them to drink.
  4. Keep cooling for 10–15 minutes, then go to the vet with the car's air-con on, even if they seem better. Damage can show up hours later.
  5. If you have a rectal thermometer, stop active cooling at about 39.5 °C so they don't get too cold.

Never leave a pet in a parked car, even with the windows open. Walk dogs early in the morning or after sunset, and keep water and shade available all day.

A5

Poisoning

Go to a vet now Poisoning

Many everyday foods, medicines and plants are toxic. Act fast, even if your pet seems fine. Signs can take hours or days.

Signs to look for

  • Vomiting, drooling or diarrhea
  • Shaking, twitching or seizures
  • Wobbly, unusually sleepy or overexcited
  • Pale gums, bleeding or bruising (rat poison)
  • Trouble breathing

Don't

  • Making your pet vomit unless a vet tells you to. It's dangerous with some poisons and if your pet is drowsy.
  • Home remedies like salt, milk, oil or hydrogen peroxide.
  • Giving any human medicine.

What to do now

  1. Move your pet away from the source and remove anything left in their mouth, if it's safe to do so.
  2. Note what they ate, about how much, and when. Bring the packaging, label or a piece of the plant.
  3. Call your vet or the nearest 24-hour clinic now and tell them you're on your way.
  4. If it's on their skin or fur (for example a flea product), wash with mild dish soap and lukewarm water, then rinse well.

Common poisons

  • Chocolate, coffee and energy drinks
  • Grapes and raisins
  • Onions, garlic and chives
  • Xylitol: sugar-free gum, candy and some peanut butters
  • Human painkillers. Paracetamol is deadly to cats; ibuprofen harms both cats and dogs.
  • Rat and mouse poison, insect sprays and pesticides
  • Lilies (cats). Even pollen or the vase water can cause kidney failure.
  • Dog flea and tick products on cats (permethrin)
  • Alcohol, cleaning products and antifreeze

A6

Toad poisoning

Go to a vet now Poisoning

Cane toads, the large brown toads that come out after rain, release a poison when a dog or cat bites or licks them.

Signs to look for

  • Sudden heavy drooling or foaming at the mouth
  • Pawing at the mouth, shaking the head
  • Bright red gums
  • Vomiting, wobbliness, a racing heartbeat, seizures

Don't

  • Spraying water down the throat. It can go into the lungs.
  • Making them vomit.

What to do now

  1. Rinse the mouth straight away. Point the head down and run a gentle stream of water across the gums and tongue from the side, so it flows out of the mouth. Keep going for 10 minutes.
  2. Wipe the gums and tongue with a damp cloth to remove the sticky poison.
  3. Go to the vet now, especially if there's anything more than drooling.

A7

Choking

Go to a vet now Breathing & choking

Something stuck in the mouth or throat. If your pet is still breathing and coughing, go to the vet. Only step in if they can't breathe.

Signs to look for

  • Pawing at the mouth, gagging or retching
  • Panic, struggling to breathe
  • Blue or pale gums
  • Collapse

Don't

  • Sweeping your fingers blindly. It can push the object deeper.
  • Pulling on string, thread or tinsel hanging from the mouth or bottom. It can cut the intestines.

What to do now

  1. Stay calm and hold them gently but firmly. A choking pet may bite.
  2. Open the mouth and look. If you can see the object and get a grip on it, remove it carefully with fingers or tweezers.
  3. If you can't remove it and they can't breathe: hold a cat or small dog with their back against your chest and give 5 firm thrusts inward and upward just below the ribs.
  4. For a large dog, put your arms around the belly from behind, make a fist just below the ribs and push firmly up and forward 5 times. If they're lying down, push from behind the ribs toward the head.
  5. Check the mouth again and repeat. If they collapse and stop breathing, start CPR.
  6. Go to the vet even if the object comes out. The throat may be injured.

A8

Trouble breathing

Go to a vet now Breathing & choking

Breathing problems can come from heart disease, asthma, injury or allergic reactions. They are always urgent.

Signs to look for

  • Open-mouth breathing in a cat
  • Neck stretched out, elbows held out, belly heaving
  • More than 40 breaths a minute at rest
  • Blue, grey or pale gums; noisy breathing

Don't

  • Holding them tightly or covering their face.
  • Offering food or water.

What to do now

  1. Keep them calm and cool, and handle them as little as possible.
  2. Loosen or remove a tight collar or harness.
  3. Let them pick the position that's comfortable. Carry small pets in an open carrier.
  4. Go to the vet now, with the car's air-con on.

A9

Bleeding & wounds

Go to a vet now Injuries & wounds

Most bleeding stops with firm, steady pressure. Bite wounds can look small but often get infected.

Signs to look for

  • Bleeding that spurts or won't stop
  • A deep or gaping wound, or one on the chest or belly
  • Puncture wounds from another animal's bite
  • Pale gums, weakness (blood loss)

Don't

  • Tourniquets, unless you're trained. They can kill the limb.
  • Hydrogen peroxide or alcohol in the wound. They damage tissue.
  • Pulling out something stuck in the wound.

What to do now

  1. If they're in pain, use a soft muzzle or towel so they don't bite. Skip this if they're vomiting or struggling to breathe.
  2. Press firmly on the wound with a clean cloth or gauze for at least 5 minutes. Don't lift it to check.
  3. If blood soaks through, add more layers on top. Don't remove the first one.
  4. Once it stops, wrap it snugly but not tight. You should fit two fingers under the bandage.
  5. Go to the vet for anything deeper than a scrape, all bite wounds, and any bleeding that won't stop.

A10

Hit by a vehicle or fall

Go to a vet now Injuries & wounds

Internal bleeding and chest injuries may not show at first. Pets that fall from balconies or windows need a vet check too.

Signs to look for

  • Limping, not putting weight on a leg
  • Fast breathing, pale gums
  • Swelling belly, bleeding from the nose or mouth
  • Seems fine but was hit or fell. Still go.

Don't

  • Giving any painkiller.
  • Trying to straighten or splint a leg.
  • Giving food or water.

What to do now

  1. Make sure you're safe from traffic first.
  2. Approach slowly and speak softly. Hurt animals bite, so use a towel or muzzle unless they're vomiting or struggling to breathe.
  3. Move them as little as possible. Slide a large dog onto a board or blanket to use as a stretcher, keeping the back and neck straight. Put small pets in a box or carrier.
  4. Press on any bleeding, keep them calm, and go to the vet now.

A11

Seizures

Go to a vet now Collapse & seizures

Most seizures stop within 2 minutes. Keep your pet safe, time it, and call your vet.

Signs to look for

  • Collapse, stiff or paddling legs
  • Jaw chomping, drooling, losing control of pee or poo
  • Not responding to you
  • Afterwards: confused, wobbly, briefly blind

Don't

  • Putting fingers or anything else in their mouth. Pets can't swallow their tongue.
  • Holding them down.
  • Giving food, water or medicine until they're fully alert.

What to do now

  1. Stay calm and note the time it starts.
  2. Move furniture and objects away, keep them away from stairs and water, and put a towel under the head. Keep your hands away from the mouth.
  3. Dim the lights and keep things quiet. Film it if you can, since it helps the vet.
  4. Afterwards, keep them somewhere quiet and cool, and speak softly while they recover.
  5. Call your vet after any first seizure. Go now if it lasts over 5 minutes, they have another one, they don't wake up between seizures, or they may have eaten a poison.

A12

Bloat (twisted stomach)

Go to a vet now Stomach & bladder Dogs only

The stomach fills with gas and can twist. It can kill within hours and usually needs surgery. Most common in large, deep-chested dogs.

Signs to look for

  • Swollen, tight belly
  • Retching or trying to vomit with nothing coming up
  • Restless, pacing, can't get comfortable
  • Drooling, pale gums, collapse

Don't

  • Waiting to see if it passes.
  • Giving food, water or home remedies.
  • Trying to make them vomit.

What to do now

  1. Go to the vet immediately. Call ahead so they can prepare.
  2. Keep your dog as calm and still as you can on the way.

To lower the risk, feed two or three smaller meals a day and rest for an hour before and after meals.

A13

Can't pee (blocked bladder)

Go to a vet now Stomach & bladder

A blocked bladder, most common in male cats, can be fatal within a day or two. It's often mistaken for constipation.

Signs to look for

  • Straining in the litter box with little or no pee
  • Going back to the box again and again
  • Crying while trying to pee, or peeing outside the box
  • Licking the genitals; vomiting, hiding, low energy

Don't

  • Pressing on the belly or bladder.
  • Waiting until the next day.

What to do now

  1. Go to the vet now. Tell them when you last saw your pet pee normally.
  2. Carry them gently in a carrier.

A14

Vomiting & diarrhea

Call your vet Stomach & bladder

One episode in a bright, adult pet is often minor. Call your vet for advice and watch closely for the warning signs below.

Signs to look for

  • Go now: repeated vomiting, or they can't keep water down
  • Go now: blood in the vomit or stool, or black, tarry stool
  • Go now: swollen or painful belly, weakness or collapse
  • Go now: a puppy or kitten, or an unvaccinated young dog (parvovirus risk)
  • Go now: may have swallowed a toy, bone or poison

Don't

  • Human anti-diarrhea or stomach medicines. Some are toxic to cats and some dogs.
  • Withholding food from a cat for more than a day. It can cause liver disease.

What to do now

  1. Call your vet and describe what you've seen. A photo helps.
  2. Offer small amounts of water often.
  3. If your vet agrees, feed small portions of bland food, like plain boiled chicken and rice for dogs.
  4. Keep a sick puppy away from other dogs and disinfect the area. Parvovirus spreads easily.

A15

Burns & electric shock

Go to a vet now Heat & burns

Chewed electric cords, hot water and chemicals are the usual causes. Electric shock can cause breathing problems hours later.

Signs to look for

  • Red, blistered or raw skin; singed fur
  • Burns at the corners of the mouth after chewing a cord
  • Coughing or trouble breathing
  • Collapse

Don't

  • Touching a pet that's still in contact with electricity.
  • Ice, butter, oil or creams on burns.
  • Popping blisters.

What to do now

  1. Electric shock: switch off the power or unplug before you touch them. If you can't, push them away with something dry and wooden, like a broom handle.
  2. Check breathing, and start CPR if they aren't breathing and have no heartbeat.
  3. Heat burns: run cool water over the area for 10 minutes, then cover with a clean, damp cloth.
  4. Chemical burns: wear gloves and rinse with plenty of running water for 15–20 minutes. Stop them licking it.
  5. Go to the vet, even if they seem fine.

A16

Snake bite

Go to a vet now Bites & stings

Venom spreads faster when your pet moves. Keep them still and get to a vet. Signs can be delayed.

Signs to look for

  • A sudden yelp, swelling, puncture marks
  • Bleeding that won't stop
  • Drooling, wide pupils, trembling
  • Weakness, wobbliness, trouble breathing, collapse

Don't

  • Cutting the wound, sucking out venom, tourniquets or ice.
  • Waiting for signs to appear.

What to do now

  1. Keep yourself safe. Don't try to catch or kill the snake. Take a photo from a distance if it's easy.
  2. Keep your pet calm and still, and carry them if you can.
  3. Take off the collar if the bite is on the head or neck, because it may swell.
  4. Go to the vet now. Call ahead so they can check for antivenom.

A17

Stings & allergic reactions

Call your vet Bites & stings

Bee, wasp, ant and centipede stings usually cause swelling that settles. A spreading reaction or trouble breathing is an emergency.

Signs to look for

  • Mild: swelling and pain where they were stung, licking the spot
  • Go now: the face swells and keeps spreading, or hives all over the body
  • Go now: vomiting, diarrhea, pale gums, weakness or collapse
  • Go now: trouble breathing, a sting inside the mouth or throat, or many stings

Don't

  • Giving human allergy or cold tablets without a vet's dose. Some contain decongestants that are toxic to pets.

What to do now

  1. Move away from the insects.
  2. If you see a bee stinger, scrape it out sideways with a card. Don't squeeze it.
  3. Hold a cold pack wrapped in cloth on the spot for 10 minutes.
  4. Watch closely for the next 1–2 hours and call your vet for advice.

A18

Eye injuries

Go to a vet now Injuries & wounds

Eyes get worse fast. Flat-faced breeds like Shih Tzus and Pugs are prone to eye injuries, and an eye can even pop out.

Signs to look for

  • Squinting, an eye held shut, pawing at it
  • Redness, tearing, cloudiness
  • Something stuck in the eye
  • An eye bulging out of its socket

Don't

  • Removing something stuck in the eye.
  • Pushing a bulging eye back in.
  • Human eye drops or ointments.

What to do now

  1. Stop them rubbing it. Use a cone collar or hold their paws.
  2. Chemicals: flush the eye with sterile saline or clean, lukewarm water for 10–15 minutes.
  3. Eye out of its socket: keep it moist with a clean cloth soaked in saline and go straight away.
  4. Go to the vet the same day for any eye injury.

A19

CPR

Go to a vet now Collapse & seizures

Only for a pet who is unconscious, not breathing and has no heartbeat. Get someone to drive to the vet while you work.

Signs to look for

  • Doesn't respond when you call or touch them
  • No chest movement for 10 seconds
  • No heartbeat on the left side of the chest, just behind the elbow

Don't

  • CPR on a pet who is breathing or has a heartbeat. It can hurt them.

What to do now

  1. Lay them on their right side on a firm surface. Put flat-chested breeds like Bulldogs on their back.
  2. Cats and small dogs: wrap one hand around the chest just behind the elbows and squeeze. Larger dogs: stack both hands on the widest part of the chest. On their back: press on the breastbone.
  3. Push hard and fast: a third to a half of the chest's width, 100–120 times a minute (the beat of "Stayin' Alive"). Let the chest come all the way back up.
  4. After 30 compressions, close the mouth, straighten the neck and give 2 quick breaths into the nose, watching the chest rise.
  5. Keep going 30 compressions to 2 breaths. Check for breathing and a heartbeat every 2 minutes, and keep going on the way to the vet.

B. Breed guide

Shown in the Breed guide and on each pet's profile. The app also uses the weight range to tell owners whether their pet is “within the typical range”. Ratings are 1 (low) to 5 (high); “Handles heat” means tolerance of hot, humid weather.

B1

Aspin (Dog)

Also called Asong Pinoy, Mixed Breed

The Philippines' own street-smart dog: alert, loyal and adaptable. Aspins are generally hardy and well suited to the local climate.

Weight8–20 kgLifespan12–16 yrs SizeMediumCoatShort, single coat

Energy 4/5 · Grooming needs 1/5 · Shedding 2/5 · Handles heat 5/5 · Good with kids 4/5

Health watch-list

  • Tick-borne blood infections (ehrlichia, babesia)
  • Mange and other skin parasites
  • Parvovirus and distemper if not vaccinated

Care tips

  • Year-round tick and flea prevention is the most important care step.
  • Keep vaccines up to date, including rabies and leptospirosis.
  • Spaying or neutering helps prevent roaming and unwanted litters.
Feeding
Two meals a day of a complete dog food for medium dogs. Rice with leftovers isn't a complete diet, and cooked bones can splinter. Keep the waist visible from above.
Exercise
Active. Aim for about an hour a day of walks and play, in the cooler morning or evening. Needs a secure fence, as Aspins like to roam.
Grooming
Low. Brush weekly with a rubber brush, bathe monthly or when dirty, and check for ticks after every walk.
Behavior
Alert, loyal and quick to learn. Often wary of strangers, which makes a good watchdog. Early socialization and treat-based training work well.

B2

Shih Tzu (Dog)

Affectionate lap dog that loves company. Its flat face and long coat need extra care in hot weather.

Weight4–7.5 kgLifespan10–16 yrs SizeSmallCoatLong, double coat

Energy 2/5 · Grooming needs 5/5 · Shedding 2/5 · Handles heat 1/5 · Good with kids 4/5

Health watch-list

  • Breathing problems from the flat face (BOAS)
  • Eye injuries, ulcers and dry eye
  • Dental disease
  • Ear infections and skin allergies

Care tips

  • Keep indoors with a fan or air-con on hot days and avoid midday walks.
  • Brush daily or keep a short puppy cut, with a groom every 4–6 weeks.
  • Wipe the eyes and face daily, and see a vet quickly for any squinting.
Feeding
Small-breed food in two or three small meals. Small kibble is easier for the flat face to pick up. Gains weight easily, so keep treats tiny.
Exercise
Low. Two short walks of 15–20 minutes plus indoor play. Never exercise in the heat; the flat face makes overheating dangerous.
Grooming
High. Brush daily, or keep a short puppy cut with a groom every 4–6 weeks. Wipe the eyes and face daily and brush the teeth often.
Behavior
Friendly, affectionate and happiest near people. Can be slow to house-train, so be patient and consistent.

Labrador Retriever (Dog)

Also called Labrador, Lab

Friendly, eager-to-please family dog with lots of energy. Labs love food, so watch their weight.

Weight25–36 kgLifespan11–14 yrs SizeLargeCoatShort, dense double coat

Energy 5/5 · Grooming needs 2/5 · Shedding 4/5 · Handles heat 3/5 · Good with kids 5/5

Health watch-list

  • Obesity
  • Hip and elbow dysplasia
  • Ear infections
  • Exercise-induced collapse

Care tips

  • Measure meals and keep treats under 10% of daily food.
  • Exercise early in the morning or evening, and let them swim if they can.
  • Dry the ears after baths and swimming.
Feeding
Large-breed food in two measured meals. Labs are very food-driven and gain weight easily, so measure portions and count treats. A slow-feeder bowl helps fast eaters.
Exercise
Very high. One to two hours a day of walks, fetch and swimming. A bored Lab chews. Rest in the shade between games on hot days.
Grooming
Moderate. Brush weekly, and daily when shedding. Dry the ears well after swimming to prevent infections.
Behavior
Friendly, eager to please and great with families. Easy to train with food rewards, but boisterous as a young dog.

Golden Retriever (Dog)

Also called Golden

Gentle, patient and very social. Their thick coat needs regular brushing and they feel the heat.

Weight25–34 kgLifespan10–12 yrs SizeLargeCoatMedium-long, double coat

Energy 4/5 · Grooming needs 3/5 · Shedding 5/5 · Handles heat 2/5 · Good with kids 5/5

Health watch-list

  • Hip and elbow dysplasia
  • Higher cancer risk than many breeds
  • Hot spots and skin allergies in humid weather
  • Hypothyroidism

Care tips

  • Brush 2–3 times a week. Don't shave the coat; it protects against sun and heat.

Feeding
Large-breed food in two measured meals, and large-breed puppy food while growing. Keep lean, as extra weight strains the hips and elbows.
Exercise
High. One to two hours a day of walks, retrieving and swimming, in the cool parts of the day.
Grooming
Moderate to high. Brush two or three times a week, and daily when shedding. Trim the feathering and check the ears weekly.
Behavior
Gentle, patient and very social. Gets on with people and other pets, but doesn't like being left alone for long.

Siberian Husky (Dog)

Also called Husky

Bred for snow, so the tropics are hard on them. Friendly, vocal and a known escape artist.

Weight16–27 kgLifespan12–14 yrs SizeMediumCoatThick, double coat

Energy 5/5 · Grooming needs 3/5 · Shedding 5/5 · Handles heat 1/5 · Good with kids 4/5

Health watch-list

  • Heatstroke
  • Eye problems (cataracts, progressive retinal atrophy)
  • Hypothyroidism

Care tips

  • Needs air-con or a cool shaded room on hot days.
  • Exercise only at dawn or after sunset, and watch for heavy panting.
  • Don't shave the coat; brush out the loose undercoat instead.
Feeding
Often eats less than you'd expect for its size. Two measured meals, adjusted to keep a lean, athletic shape.
Exercise
Very high, but only in the cool early morning or late evening here. Keep in a cool room or air-con during the day. Walk on a leash; Huskies run off and escape fences.
Grooming
Brush two or three times a week, and daily when shedding. Don't shave the double coat; it protects the skin from sun.
Behavior
Friendly, playful and independent, so not a guard dog. Howls and "talks". Strong urge to chase cats and small animals.

Pug (Dog)

Charming, comical companion. The flat face makes breathing and staying cool difficult.

Weight6–8 kgLifespan12–15 yrs SizeSmallCoatShort, fine coat

Energy 2/5 · Grooming needs 2/5 · Shedding 4/5 · Handles heat 1/5 · Good with kids 5/5

Health watch-list

  • Breathing problems (BOAS)
  • Heatstroke
  • Eye injuries
  • Skin-fold infections
  • Obesity

Care tips

  • Keep cool and indoors on hot days, and keep walks short and gentle.

Small portions in two meals. Pugs overeat easily, and extra weight makes breathing worse. Use treats sparingly.

Low to moderate. Two short walks of 15–20 minutes, in cool hours only. Stop at the first sign of heavy panting, and use a harness, not a collar.
Grooming
Low to moderate. Brush weekly, as Pugs shed a lot. Clean the face wrinkles daily and dry them well.
Behavior
Charming, playful and people-focused. Good with children. Snores, and can be stubborn.

D2

Example answer: “How often should I deworm my puppy?”

Puppies pick up worms easily, so they're dewormed more often than adults. A common schedule is:

  • Every 2 weeks from about 2 weeks old until 12 weeks
  • Then monthly until 6 months old
  • Then every 3 months for adults

Your vet will choose the right product and dose for your puppy's weight, so ask them before giving anything. Keep the yard clean and treat fleas too, since fleas can spread tapeworm.

D3

Example answer: “My dog ate a chocolate bar. What should I do?”

Chocolate is poisonous to dogs, especially dark and baking chocolate. Call or go to a vet now, even if your dog seems fine, because signs can take hours to show.

While you get there:

  • Note the type of chocolate, how much, and when it was eaten
  • Bring the wrapper if you can
  • Don't make your dog vomit unless the vet tells you to

Watch for vomiting, restlessness, fast breathing, shaking or seizures, and tell the vet straight away if you see them.

D4

Example answer: “How do I keep my cat cool during hot days?”

Cats handle heat better than many dogs, but hot, humid days can still be dangerous.

  • Keep fresh water in several places, and add wet food for extra fluids
  • Give them a shaded, airy spot, and use a fan or air-con in the hottest hours
  • Brush long-haired cats often to remove loose fur
  • Never leave a cat in a parked car

Open-mouth panting, drooling, wobbliness or collapse can mean heatstroke. Move them somewhere cool and go to a vet now.

Example answer: “My male cat keeps going to the litter box but nothing comes out.”

This can be a blocked bladder, which is an emergency in male cats. Take him to a vet now. A blockage can become life-threatening within a day.

D8

Example answer: “Can I give my dog paracetamol for pain?”

Please don't give human pain medicine to your dog without your vet's advice. Many common ones, including paracetamol and ibuprofen, can cause serious harm to dogs, and paracetamol is deadly to cats.

Call your vet and describe where your dog seems sore and since when. They can examine your dog and prescribe a pain medicine that's safe for pets, at the right dose for their weight.

Example answer: “How often should I bathe my Shih Tzu?”

Most Shih Tzus do well with a bath every 3 to 4 weeks. Bathing too often can dry out their skin.

  • Use a dog shampoo, not human shampoo
  • Brush before the bath to remove tangles, then dry them fully, especially the ears
  • Wipe the eyes and face daily, as tear stains and food build up there

Many owners keep a short "puppy cut" with a groom every 4 to 6 weeks, which makes daily care much easier.
